## Which Construct plan should you start on?

Construct's paid plans are a fixed monthly price with published plan limits: Lite at $9, Starter at $59, and Pro at $299. Billed annually, they work out to $7.50, $39, and $199 a month. Pro has a 7-day trial. Current prices and limits are on the [pricing page](/pricing/).

The plans are not priced by hours of work. They differ in usage and limits:

That is why the calculator shows all three plans side by side. Read your result this way:

- **Start on Lite** if you are moving up to three recurring jobs, such as a daily inbox summary, a weekly report, and a CRM update, and each one fits in 50 steps. It has the largest saving in the worked example below.
- **Start on Starter** if the VA's suitable share covers more than three recurring jobs, a task needs up to 150 steps, or you want separate agents for separate areas.
- **Start on Pro** for long research or build runs of up to 1,000 steps, many scheduled jobs, or your own model keys. Take the 7-day trial first to see whether you need it.

If your result shows Lite saving the most but you are unsure your workload fits its usage, start there anyway and move up when you hit the limit.

## How the calculator works

The model is deliberately simple, so you can check every number it gives you.

1. **Monthly VA cost** is hours per week times hourly rate times 52 weeks divided by 12 months, about 4.33 weeks a month.
2. **Tasks per month** is the VA's monthly minutes divided by the average minutes per task. This is only used to count review time.
3. **The agent's share** moves that percentage of tasks, and the same percentage of the VA bill, off the VA. The rest stays with the VA.
4. **Review time** is the agent's tasks per month times your review minutes per task.
5. **Monthly total** for each plan is the remaining VA cost, plus the plan price, plus review time priced at your hourly value if you enter one.

If checking a task takes as long as doing it, the calculator says so: at that point the agent saves no human time, whatever the dollar figures show.

## A worked example, and what moves the answer

Take the default inputs, which are illustrative: 20 VA hours a week at $15, 20 minutes per task, 30% of tasks suited to an agent, and 3 minutes to review each agent task. That is 260 tasks a month, 78 of them handed to the agent, and 3.9 hours of review.

Now change one input at a time in the calculator and watch what happens.

**Review time is the input that flips the answer.** Price your own time at an illustrative $50 an hour and the 3.9 review hours cost $195, so Starter's saving falls from $331 to $136 and Pro costs $104 more than the VA alone. Push review to 10 minutes per task and every plan costs more than keeping the VA. At $50 an hour and 78 tasks, each extra minute of review per task costs about $65 a month, so Starter stops saving money at a little over 5 minutes of review per task.

**The VA's rate sets the ceiling.** At the bottom of the offshore agency range, $4 an hour, the whole 20-hour engagement costs about $347 a month and the agent-suited share is worth $104. Lite still saves money on paper; Pro costs $195 more. At the top of the US or UK freelance range, $40 an hour, the agent-suited share is worth $1,040 a month, and even Pro saves $741 before review.

**Small engagements leave little to save.** At 5 hours a week, a 30% share is worth about $97 a month. Lite and Starter come out ahead before review; Pro does not.

The pattern: an agent saves the most when the VA is expensive, the suitable share is large, and the result is quick to check. It saves little or nothing when the VA is cheap, the work is mostly judgment, or every result needs a careful read.

## Which tasks count as suitable for an agent?

This is the input most people overestimate. A task is a good candidate when it is recurring, follows a procedure you could write down, and produces something quick to check: an inbox sorted into labels, a daily summary posted to Slack, a spreadsheet of companies with sources, CRM records updated from a form. A task is a poor candidate when it depends on a relationship, on reading someone's mood, or on judgment calls nobody has written down.

[AI agent vs virtual assistant](/blog/ai-agent-vs-virtual-assistant/) goes through where each one wins, and [the AI delegation checklist](/blog/ai-delegation-checklist/) is a practical way to sort a VA's task list into "agent", "VA", and "not yet". If you are unsure, enter a low share first. A 15% share you can actually move is worth more than a 50% share you cannot.

Construct does this kind of work from its own cloud workspace: it has a native agent email inbox, works in Slack, Telegram, Discord slash commands, and the web, connects tools from the live integration catalog, and runs recurring jobs on a schedule.

## Before you start

- **Keep sign-offs with a person.** Construct's workflows are linear and do not pause for approval yet, so for anything customer-facing, ask for drafts and have you or your VA send them.
- **Measure review time on real runs.** Run a candidate task on demand a few times before you trust the calculator's review input; the next section shows how.
- **Size jobs to the plan.** If a task stops short of Lite's 50 steps per task, split it into smaller jobs or move to Starter's 150. Work finished before a stop is kept in the workspace.

## How long does reviewing agent work take?

There is no published benchmark for your tasks, so measure it. Run a candidate task on demand a handful of times, time how long it takes you to check each result, and use the median. Expect review to be longer in the first weeks and shorter once you trust a job's output.

Construct's Activity feed records each action with a short reason, and chat keeps tool records, which makes it quicker to see what the agent did than reading back through a transcript. They are useful operating records, not a complete audit log, and they do not replace actually checking the output. Our article on [the verification gap](/blog/agent-verification-gap/) explains why checking, not writing, is usually the real cost.

Long jobs add one more cost the calculator does not model: retries. An agent that finishes a long task only part of the time needs extra runs, and sometimes extra checking. The [agent reliability calculator](/blog/agent-reliability-calculator/) estimates that from per-step success and step count.

## What the calculator leaves out

Read the result as a first-pass estimate. It does not include:

- **Setup time.** Writing instructions, connecting tools, and supervising the first runs takes hours up front.
- **Whether you can actually cut the VA's hours.** Agencies often sell fixed packages. If your VA engagement is 20 hours or nothing, moving 30% of tasks frees VA time for other work rather than lowering the bill.
- **Plan fit.** The calculator does not check whether your workload fits a plan's usage or steps per task.
- **Quality differences.** A VA who catches an awkward client email before it goes out is doing work no line in this calculator captures.
- **Hiring costs on either side,** such as recruiting, onboarding, and turnover for a VA.

## Running both together

The answer does not have to be "VA or agent"; it can be both: the agent takes the recurring, checkable work, and the VA keeps the relationship work and becomes the person who reviews what the agent produces. If your VA does the review, enter their hourly rate as the reviewer's value and the calculator prices it correctly.
